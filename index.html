Na trí chéim — comparison

Three degrees, three names, and the names themselves tell you what they do: bunchéim (base step), breischéim (increase step), sárchéim (surpassing step).

CéimLáithreachCaite / coinníollach
Bunchéimmaithmaith
Breischéimníos fearrní ba fhearr
Sárchéimis fearrba fhearr → ab fhearr
Where níos comes from. It is a worn-down ní is — “the thing that is”. That is why the past form is ní ba: the is simply becomes ba. Once you see the join, the odd-looking past comparative stops being arbitrary. Before a vowel or fh, ba → ab: ab fhearr, ab óige, ab áille.
  • Copula: Is fearr tusa ná mise — tighter, more emphatic.
  • Tá: Tá tusa níos fearr ná mise — plainer.
  • Past copula: Ba dheacra an rang sin ná rang Airt.
  • Past tá: Bhí an rang sin ní ba dheacra ná rang Airt.
  • Superlative: Is é seo an rang is deacra → Ba é sin an rang ba dheacra.
  • Ulster ranking with bí ar: tá sé ar an fhear is láidre; “one of” = ar dhuine de.

Common irregulars: maith → fearr, olc → measa, mór → mó, beag → lú, furasta → fusa, fada → faide, gearr → giorra.

Dóigh · Áit · Fáth · Treo · Am

These five cén words normally take the indirect relative: a + eclipsis in the present and future, ar + lenition in the past.

CeistSampla

The wrinkle. Am and uair are also commonly heard with the direct relative — cén t-am a bheas an rang thart?, an uair a thuig mé. Both are in use. The other four are solidly indirect. Ulster cad chuige behaves exactly like cén fáth: cad chuige ar luaigh sé iad?

Verbs of motion + a + lenited verbal noun

In Ulster, a verb of motion is followed by a and a lenited verbal noun, where the standard often uses ag. The a is a worn-down do, and the pattern is the older of the two.

  • Téigh — Chuaigh mé a shiopadóireacht.
  • Tar — Tháinig mé anseo a fhoghlaim Gaeilge.
  • Imigh — D'imigh sé a thógáil tí.
  • Tosaigh — Thosaigh sé a chur ceiste.

Note the genitive on the noun after the verbal noun: tógáil tí, cur ceiste. Ulster also uses ag gabháil where the standard has ag dul: tá mé ag gabháil do do phógadh.

An ‘a’ na huilíochta — “all that”

A special relative a meaning all that, taking eclipsis. It makes a whole clause into a quantity.

  • Sin a dtuigim — that's all I understand.
  • Sin a ndeir sé — that's all he says.
  • A bhfuil agam — all that I have.
  • With de in the past: dá bhfaca mé, dár casadh orm riamh — of all I ever saw / ever met.

So: Sin an scannán ba mheasa dá bhfaca mé = that's the worst film of all I ever saw.

Feelings sit on you

English says “I am proud”. Irish says the pride is on you. The feeling is a noun, and each one takes its own second preposition for the cause — these are not predictable and have to be learned as pairs.

Na treonna — why the compass works

Irish direction words are built from a person facing the rising sun. East is in front — oirthear, from oir (front). West is behind — iarthar, from iar (after, behind). South is on the right — deisceart, from deas (right). North is on the left — tuaisceart, from tuath (left). The compass is a human body turned to the dawn, and deas is also why deis means both the right-hand side and an opportunity.

Motion is built in. Siar = to the west, aniar = from the west; soir = to the east, anoir = from the east; ó dheas, aneas; ó thuaidh, aduaidh. So gaoth aniar is a west wind — one blowing from the west. And i bhfad siar means long ago: Irish lays time out east to west, with the past in the west.

Gall. The word started as “a Gaul”, then came to mean any foreigner — first the Norse, later the Anglo-Normans and English. That drift gives Dubhghaill and Fionnghaill (dark and fair foreigners, i.e. Danes and Norwegians), which survive in the surname Ó Dubhghaill (Doyle) and the place-name Fine Gall (Fingal). An dá réimniú — the two conjugations

Two conjugations, plus eleven irregular verbs. The quickest test is the future.

The test: an f in the future means first conjugation; a long ó/eo means second. Ulster speech often keeps the older -óchaidh / -eochaidh: cheannóchadh sé, d'imeochadh sé.

The eleven irregulars — bí, abair, beir, clois/cluin, déan, faigh, feic, ith, tabhair, tar, téigh — are the verbs you use most. High-frequency verbs resist being tidied up; that is true in every language with irregular verbs.

Má agus dá

  • Má = if, for something real or open. Ordinary tenses, lenition: má thuigeann tú, má bhí sé ann. Never a future — use the present: má thagann sé amárach.
  • Mura (+ eclipsis) is its negative: mura dtuigeann tú. Past murar + lenition: murar thuig siad.
  • Dá = if, for something unreal. Eclipsis + conditional: dá mbeadh an t-airgead agam, cheannóinn é.
  • Copula: más for the real case, dá mba for the unreal.

Quick test: if English can say “if … were”, you need dá. If it can say “if … is / was”, you need má.
